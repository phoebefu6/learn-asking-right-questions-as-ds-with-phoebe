Learn Asking the Right Questions as a Data Scientist with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

The gap, from the scientist's chair

An operations director asks whether you can predict which outlets are going to run out of stock. A data scientist hears a classification problem and starts asking about labels and class balance. Six weeks later there is a model, it scores well on a holdout, and nobody acts on it, because what she wanted was fewer complaint calls about empty chronic-medication shelves, the model predicted something slightly different, and the history it needed was never recorded. Neither of them was careless. They used the same word for different things. Tonight is about noticing that in the room: why the word "predict" opens a gap every time, the door a scientist should open first and widest, and the one sentence that lets her learn how prediction work thinks while she is answering you.

Part 0

Where we start

A prediction is only worth something when a person does something different because of it, and most predictions that go unused were lost in the first meeting, before anyone opened a notebook. The running case for all six sessions is Harbourline, an invented chain of 38 pharmacy and clinic outlets across Singapore and Malaysia, and its operations director Adeline Tan. She has never heard the words target variable and does not need to. She has a request: "The board keeps asking about AI. Can we predict which outlets are going to run out of stock?" You have 45 minutes with her. Everything in the six sessions is about what you ask in those minutes, and in what order.

Part 1 · covers why the gap opens

Two tunes, and the word predict

5 min live

In 1990 a Stanford psychology student, Elizabeth Newton, split people into tappers and listeners. Tappers picked a well-known song and tapped its rhythm on a table; listeners had to name it. Before tapping, tappers guessed listeners would get about half. Listeners got 3 of 120. The tapper hears the whole melody behind every knock and cannot switch it off. In a prediction meeting the knock is one word, "predict", and each side hears a different song behind it.

LiveThe tune in your head, heard from the scientist's side3 min▶

Newton's tappers were not bad at tapping. They could not un-know the song while they tapped, so they judged the listener's chances from inside their own experience. Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber had named this a year earlier: the curse of knowledge, the finding that better-informed people cannot fully set their information aside when predicting what less-informed people will understand.

A data scientist carries a very loud tune. "Predict" arrives with a whole melody attached: a target variable, a row per outlet per day, a probability, a threshold, a score on held-out data. Adeline's tune is just as loud and completely different. To her, "predict" means "stop getting caught by empty shelves": a patient on chronic medication turns up, the shelf is empty, the patient goes to a competitor and the complaint comes to her. She has no reason to say which product, how often, or what an over-order costs, because inside her tune those are obvious.

So the scientist fills the silence with his own tune, and the model gets built to it. The gap is not closed by teaching her the vocabulary. It is closed by questions that make her tune audible: what exactly runs out, how often, and what each kind of mistake costs, asked in words that need no translation, with the reason for asking said out loud.

Self-studyWhy "AI" is the loudest knock in the room2 min▶

Her request starts with the board: "The board keeps asking about AI." Behind that sentence, on her side of the table, sit two different projects. The board wants the word AI on a slide. She wants fewer calls. A scientist who hears only the first builds something impressive for a board meeting; one who hears only the second might solve it with a spreadsheet and leave her with nothing to show upward.

Neither is wrong, but they are different builds with different tests, and she will not separate them for you unless you ask. The professional move is to name both out loud early ("it sounds like there is a board question and an operations question; can we take the operations one first?") and let her choose which one the first project serves. Session 5 comes back to this when the three candidate projects are drawn, because one of them is usually not a model at all.

Real world

The forecast that forecast the wrong thing. A constructed case, but a shape every scientist will recognise. Last year a vendor sold Harbourline an "AI demand forecast". It forecast sales, per product, per outlet, and by the vendor's own measures it was fine. Nobody used it, because sales were never the problem: the problem was a shelf that is empty on the day a chronic patient comes in, and a sales forecast does not say that. The vendor heard "predict" and built the loudest tune in their own head. Adeline mentions this only once she trusts you, and only if you ask whether she has tried anything like this before.

Part 2 · covers the widest door

The outcome door, opened widest

6 min live

Every discovery meeting walks the same corridor of six doors: Outcome · Decision · Current way · Data state · Constraints · Success and partnership. The corridor, with the question that opens each door, is drawn once, in session 1 of the analyst's sibling ↗; read it there. What differs for a scientist is which door opens widest. Your output is a prediction somebody acts on, and the three facts that decide whether it is worth building at all sit behind door 1: the exact target, how often it happens today, and what each kind of wrong guess costs.

LiveThe exact target: stock-outs, not sales2 min▶

The first fact is the thing that goes wrong, named precisely enough that you could tell whether it happened yesterday. At Harbourline it is a stock-out of one of about 40 chronic-medication products at outlet level: a patient turns up and the shelf is empty. Not sales, not demand in general, not every product in the shop. That sentence already scopes the model to about 40 products across 38 outlets, and it rules out the vendor's forecast before you have seen a row of data.

The question that gets it is plain: "What exactly runs out? Which products, which outlets, and how do you find out it happened?" The last clause matters most. How she finds out is how the target is recorded, and at Harbourline the answer is that nobody records a stock-out anywhere. The only record is the complaint call. You have just learned that your label does not exist yet, in the words of a person who has never heard the word label.

LiveThe base rate: how often, and why you ask it early2 min▶

The second fact is how often it happens today. Adeline's answer is roughly 30 a month across the 38 outlets, and she knows because each one becomes a complaint call to her. Do the arithmetic on the constructed sheet: about 40 products in 38 outlets is 1,520 outlet-product pairs, and 30 a month is about seven a week. In any given week, well over 99 percent of pairs do not run out. A model that answers "never" to every one of them is right more than 99 percent of the time and useless every time.

That is why the base rate is asked early, before anyone mentions accuracy. It is the first number any prediction is judged against, and on a rare event it tells you in advance that a single accuracy figure will flatter whatever you build. It also tells you what a real improvement looks like: fewer of her 30 calls, not a higher score.

Douglas Hubbard's definition of measurement is the reason this belongs at door 1 rather than in the model review: a measurement is a quantitatively expressed reduction of uncertainty based on one or more observations. The uncertainty here is hers, about which shelves will be empty next week. A prediction that does not reduce it has measured nothing, however well it scores.

LiveTwo wrong guesses, two different bills2 min▶

The third fact is what each kind of wrong guess costs, and the two are never the same size. At Harbourline a missed stock-out costs a patient a trip to a competitor and costs Adeline a complaint to head office. An over-order costs expiry write-off on medication that sits too long, and head office measures her on write-off. A model that flags every outlet every week would stop the stock-outs and wreck the number she is judged by.

You do not need the cost matrix as a formal object in the first meeting, and she should never hear the word. You need the two bills in her words, and ideally which one she would rather pay more of. "When it happens, what does it cost you? And what does it cost when an outlet orders too much?" Treating the ratio between those costs as a design decision in its own right is the subject of Decision Intelligence ↗; here the job is only to get both numbers onto your sheet before anyone says "optimise for accuracy".

Self-studyThe non-ML answer first, and success defined before building3 min▶

Google's machine learning problem-framing course gives two pieces of advice that belong in the first meeting, not the project plan: check whether a non-ML solution solves the problem before building a model, and define the success metrics before building. Both are outcome-door questions, and both come back with facts that change the project.

The non-ML solution at Harbourline already exists. Today the pharmacist looks at the shelf and WhatsApps the area manager, and that catches most of them. Any model has to beat a person looking at a shelf, so the current way is not a nuisance to be replaced; it is the baseline you are measured against, and it is door 3. The success metric exists too, in her words: the complaint calls about empty shelves go down, and she counts them herself. She does not care what the accuracy number is. Agreeing that test in the first meeting is what stops a later argument about precision and recall that she cannot follow and should not have to.

Sharpening a question once it has been handed to you, and recognising when no column in any system answers it, is taught in Data Thinking ↗. The skill here comes one step earlier: getting the question out of a person who did not know she was asking it.

Real world

A base rate made of phone calls. Still the constructed case. Adeline's 30 a month is the only count of stock-outs Harbourline has, and it is a count of complaints. Whether every empty shelf becomes a call, or only the ones where a patient was angry enough to ring, is a question to ask her, not an assumption to build on. Either way, the finding changes the first project: a pharmacist in Johor has offered to log stock-outs every day if someone gives her a sheet, and a model that must learn from stock-outs has to start with somebody writing them down.

Open door 1 widest, then walk the rest in order. Session 3 counts what the order is worth on the bench: the same questions in any order at all average 27.5 points (anywhere from 0 to 46), the doors in their printed order score 46, and the doors tuned outcome first for a scientist score 55. The claim is the ordering and the mechanism, never the numbers as facts about a real business.

Part 3 · covers the teach-back line

The teach-back line for a scientist

4 min live

Every open question in the bank comes with a second sentence: a plain statement of why you are asking it. Said out loud, it does three things. It turns the question into a reason rather than an interrogation, it earns a little trust, and it teaches her one true thing about how prediction work is decided. By the end of 45 minutes she understands why accuracy is the wrong test for a rare event, without ever having seen the word.

LiveThree lines for the outcome door2 min▶

A teach-back line is plain (no word she would have to ask about, so "how often" not "base rate", "a wrong guess" not "a false positive"), true (a real principle of the craft, not a pleasantry) and one sentence, or two short ones at most, because a paragraph is a lecture and she stops answering.

The three outcome questions in the bank each carry one, and they are worth learning word for word. Before "What exactly runs out?": "I need the exact thing that goes wrong before I can promise to predict it. Sales and stock-outs are different questions with different data." Before "Roughly how often does it happen in a month?": "How often it happens is the first number any prediction is judged against. If it is rare, a model that says 'never' looks accurate and is useless." Before "What does it cost you, and what does it cost when an outlet orders too much?": "A wrong guess in each direction costs something different, and the model has to be built to the ratio between them, not to a single accuracy number."

Between them, those three sentences teach her the target, the base rate and the cost ratio, the three ideas a scientist most needs a sponsor to understand, and none of them uses a word from a textbook. Session 4 builds more of these and shows how to pick the one concept per meeting worth explaining properly.

Self-studyFive kinds of question, and the one that writes a wrong target into your notes2 min▶

Session 3 sorts every question in the bank into five kinds. Open questions about the past or present uncover facts and earn trust. Closed yes-or-no questions are cheap and get one fact at most. Vague ones ("what would you like AI to do for you?") cost minutes and bring back a generic. Jargon costs twice: "what is the class imbalance on the positive label?" gets nothing, and she is a little less sure you understand pharmacies. Leading questions are the dangerous kind. "So you want to forecast sales, right?" gets a polite yes, and "the target is sales" goes into your notes and later into your build. The fast yes-or-no run on the bench leaves five false beliefs like that behind it.

The trust is not decoration. Six of the 24 facts on Adeline's sheet only come out once some trust exists, including how often stock-outs happen and that the stock spreadsheet is overwritten every Friday. The most sensitive one needs the most trust of all, and it decides whether anything you build survives the quarter; session 3 shows which it is and what it takes to hear it.

Real world

The test she already had. Constructed, like every Harbourline scene. Asked "how would you know it worked?" early, with the line "if we agree on the test now, neither of us argues about it later", Adeline answers at once: the complaint calls about empty shelves go down, and she counts them herself. Asked late, after twenty minutes of precision, recall and thresholds, the same question tends to get "whatever you think is best", and the project ends up judged on a number she never chose.

Build-along

Your Six Doors sheet, for "can we predict it"

★ 22 min · everyone writes

One page, six rows, the outcome row written widest. Take a real prediction request from your own inbox, or use Adeline's if you do not have one, and build the sheet you will carry into the meeting. Every line is written before the meeting; the meeting is for listening.

Write the request as it was received, in their words. Not your translation into a modelling problem. If it arrived as "can we predict it", write "can we predict it". You will want this sentence back at the end of the meeting, to see how far the real request was from it.

★ Step 1 · the requestRequest, verbatim: "The board keeps asking about AI. Can we predict which outlets are going to run out of stock? We keep getting caught." From: Adeline Tan, operations director Note to self: two projects may be hiding in here, a board slide and fewer calls

Write the outcome row first, and give it three questions. The exact target, how often, and what each wrong guess costs. Past or present tense, nothing with "would you", nothing she can answer with yes, and no word she would have to ask about.

★ Step 2 · door 1, outcome, three questionsWhat exactly runs out? Which products, which outlets, and how do you find out it happened? Roughly how often does it happen in a month, across all the outlets? When it happens, what does it cost you? And what does it cost when an outlet orders too much?
The same door, as it is usually openedWhat is the class imbalance on the positive label? Do you need precision or recall optimised, and at what threshold?

Two open questions behind each of the other five doors, each with its teach-back line. Plain, true, one sentence. Read each pair aloud once; if the second sentence has a word she would have to ask about, change the word.

★ Step 3 · two pairs, doors 2 and 3Q: If you knew a week ahead, what would you actually do differently? Why I am asking: A prediction only pays for itself if there is an action on the other side of it. I am asking for the action first. Q: How do you catch stock-outs today, before they happen? Why I am asking: Whatever you do today is the baseline any model has to beat. I write it down before I build anything.

Mark the order you will walk. For a scientist, door 1 is opened first and widest: your output is a prediction somebody acts on, and the target, the base rate and the two costs are the facts you cannot build without. Then the action, then today's baseline, and only then the data. Session 3 measures what that order is worth. If you are an analyst, an engineer or building with AI, the sibling for your role marks a different door.

★ Step 4 · the order for tonight1 Outcome (widest, three questions) -> 2 Decision -> 3 Current way -> 4 Data state -> 6 Success -> 5 Constraints Budget: 45 minutes. About ten open questions fit. The sheet has thirteen; the last three are spares.

Rehearse the opener against Adeline. Her first line is on the page. Say your first question out loud, with its teach-back. If it took more than twenty seconds, it is two questions; split it.

★ Step 5 · the first thirty secondsAdeline: "Thanks for coming. The board keeps asking about AI. Can we predict which outlets are going to run out of stock? We keep getting caught." You: "Before we get to prediction, can I ask what exactly runs out? Which products, which outlets, and how do you find out it happened? I need the exact thing that goes wrong before I can promise to predict it. Sales and stock-outs are different questions with different data."

Keep the sheet after the meeting. Session 2 fills in the data-state row properly, session 5 turns her answers into three candidate projects, and session 6 turns the filled-in sheet into the one-page brief you send her within 24 hours. The same page carries all of it.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Finish the sheet for your real request: three outcome questions, ten more behind the other doors, a teach-back line on each, and the order marked. Bring it printed; session 3 puts it on the bench.
  2. Write the three outcome facts as you guess them. For your request, write the target, the base rate and the two costs you currently believe. Mark each one "heard from them" or "assumed by me". Most people find all three are assumed.
  3. Re-read a model you shipped that nobody used. Write down what it predicted, and then what the person who asked for it actually wanted to stop happening. If those are two different sentences, write which question would have caught it.
  4. Find the baseline. For your request, find out how the thing is caught today without any model: who looks at what, and how often it works. Session 2 starts from what has been recorded, and the current way is usually where the only record lives.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why is "predict" a trap word in a first meeting?

The curse of knowledge runs both ways. Neither side is careless and neither notices, which is why the outcome door is opened with plain questions rather than trusted to a shared word.

2 · Which three facts sit behind the outcome door for a scientist?

Without all three, no model can be scoped: you do not know what to predict, what it will be judged against, or which mistake to lean away from. Data sources and budgets matter too, but they sit behind later doors.

3 · Why is the base rate asked early, before anyone mentions accuracy?

On the constructed sheet, about seven stock-outs a week across roughly 1,520 outlet-product pairs means well over 99 percent of pairs never run out in a given week. "Never" scores brilliantly and catches nothing.
